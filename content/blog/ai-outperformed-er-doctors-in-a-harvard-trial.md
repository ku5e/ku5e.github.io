Harvard ran a controlled trial of AI performance in emergency triage and published the results this week. The AI outperformed emergency physicians on diagnostic accuracy.

Most of the conversations that follow a result like this focus immediately on liability. That conversation is worth having. It is not the most important one.

***

## What Emergency Triage Actually Tests

Emergency triage is decision-making under a specific set of conditions: incomplete information, time pressure, high consequence, and compounding cognitive load from case volume. A physician who has seen 40 patients in a shift is making probabilistic judgments under fatigue in a way that a physician at the start of a shift is not.

AI models do not fatigue. They do not anchor on the last case. They apply the same pattern recognition to the 40th patient as the first. In clinical settings specifically characterized by the conditions that degrade human decision-making over time, this is not a minor edge.

***

## The Liability Framing and What It Misses

The liability framing is: if AI gets it wrong, who is responsible? That question is real and unresolved. Hospital systems, insurers, and regulators are working through it, and they will be for years.

What it displaces: if AI gets it right at a higher rate than the physician, and the physician is the only option because the AI was not adopted, what is the liability for that outcome?

That second question does not have a clean legal form yet. It will. Performance data accumulates faster than legal frameworks move, and Harvard is not the first study to show AI outperforming physicians in specific diagnostic contexts. It will not be the last.

***

## What This Is Not

It is not a general argument that AI should replace physicians. Emergency triage is a narrow, well-defined task with structured inputs and measurable outcomes. Those conditions are what made a controlled trial possible.

The trial result does not transfer cleanly to complex differential diagnosis, treatment planning, or the relational aspects of patient care where physician judgment carries weight beyond accuracy. It is an argument that the specific task of emergency triage, under controlled conditions, is one where current AI systems demonstrably outperform humans on the metric that matters most: diagnostic accuracy.

That is a different claim, and a more defensible one.

***

## The Contrast That Sticks

Most AI news this week covered trying on clothes in your Google Photos library, AI-generated spreadsheets, and voice agents with lower latency for customer phone lines.

Those are fine. They are not what anyone argues AI is for when the conversation gets serious.

The Harvard trial is that argument: diagnostic accuracy in emergency medicine, cancer detection from routine scans three years before clinical presentation, pattern recognition applied to problems where human performance has a known ceiling and the cost of error is a life. This is where the capability matters most and where the public conversation underinvests its attention.
